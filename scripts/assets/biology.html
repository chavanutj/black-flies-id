Black fly biology and disease

Black flies (family Simuliidae) are small, stout-bodied biting flies whose larvae and pupae develop exclusively in running water. Females of several species transmit Onchocerca volvulus (the cause of human river blindness) and blood parasites of birds and livestock. This page summarises their global biology, classification, medical and veterinary importance, and the life stages used in identification.

What are black flies?

  • Insects of the order Diptera (suborder Nematocera, infraorder Culicomorpha), family Simuliidae (Adler 2026). Some common names refer to single species, for example the Blandford fly, a name strictly for Simulium posticatum (Adler 2026).
  • Small, dark and humpbacked flies (Aupalee et al. 2024) with short, stout antennae and broad, clear wings.
  • Only females take blood, usually from birds or mammals including humans (Aupalee et al. 2024). Males do not bite.
  • Distributed across all major zoogeographical regions wherever flowing water supports the aquatic immature stages, from tiny spring-fed trickles to large rivers (Srisuka et al. 2017; Adler 2026).
  • Widely recognised as the third most important group of arthropod disease vectors worldwide, although only about 10–20% of named species are important pests of humans and animals (Aupalee et al. 2026).

Classification

The World Blackflies inventory lists 2,439 valid species worldwide (2,422 living and 17 fossil), up from 2,015 living species in 2008 (Adler 2026). Living species are classified into two subfamilies, two tribes and 31 extant genera (Adler 2026):

Subfamily Parasimuliinae1 (Parasimulium)4Western North America.
Subfamily Simuliinae, tribe Prosimuliini6 (Prosimulium, Helodon, Gymnopais, Twinnia, Levitinia, Urosimulium)146Mainly Northern Hemisphere (Dumbleton 1963). Prosimulium (82 species) and Helodon (36) contain most species.
Subfamily Simuliinae, tribe Simuliini24 (led by Simulium, Gigantodax, Metacnephia, Austrosimulium, Sulcicnephia)2,272Cosmopolitan. The single genus Simulium includes 1,993 living species (82.3% of the family) in 42 subgenera (plus 12 unplaced), led by subgenera Simulium (557), Gomphostilbia (311), Nevermannia (248) and Montisimulium (81).

In the regional breakdown retained in the World Blackflies inventory, living species are distributed across the major zoogeographical regions approximately as follows: Palearctic 33%, Oriental 17%, Neotropical 16%, Australasian 12%, Afrotropical 11% and Nearctic 11% (Adler 2026). The inventory warns that these shares reflect differences in taxonomic effort and approach between regions as well as real diversity. Subspecies are rarely recognised in modern black fly taxonomy; instead, sibling taxa are tracked as species complexes and cytoforms (Adler 2026). Life cycle

Black flies are holometabolous insects whose immature stages are strictly aquatic and rheophilic, depending on flowing water (Srisuka et al. 2017).

  1. Eggs: laid in or near running water. In cold climates some species overwinter in the egg stage.
  2. Larvae: Attach to submerged stones, leaves, roots and trailing vegetation in swift to slow currents using a posterior ring of tiny hooks pressed into a silk pad (Srisuka et al. 2017). Most species are filter feeders, opening a pair of comb-like labral fans into the flow to capture fine organic detritus, bacteria and algae.
  3. Pupae: Do not feed. The mature larva spins a silk cocoon (wall-pocket, slipper-, shoe- or boot-shaped) glued to the substrate (Takaoka et al. 2019). A pair of branching thoracic gill filaments projects from the cocoon opening to extract dissolved oxygen from the water.
  4. Adults: emerge from the pupa under water and rise to the surface. Females of many species then seek a blood meal before laying eggs.

Habitat choice is strongly structured by stream size, current speed, substrate, elevation and season, so neighbouring streams or elevations often hold distinct species assemblages (Srisuka et al. 2017).

Blood feeding

Unlike mosquitoes, which pierce capillaries directly, black flies are pool feeders (telmophages). Females cut the host skin with toothed mandibles and maxillae and feed from the small pool of blood that forms. Black flies bite by day, mostly outdoors.

Host preferences vary markedly across lineages: many species feed on birds (ornithophilic), whereas others feed on mammals including livestock, wildlife and humans (Srisuka et al. 2017; Aupalee et al. 2024). In humans, bites can cause irritation, pain, swelling and redness, due to an IgE-mediated reaction to salivary gland proteins (Aupalee et al. 2024).

River blindness (onchocerciasis)

Caused by Onchocerca volvulus; at least 26 Simulium species are regarded as its vectors (Aupalee et al. 2026). It is the second leading cause of infectious blindness worldwide and one of the 21 neglected tropical diseases that WHO has targeted for elimination by 2030 (Aupalee et al. 2024).

Animal filariae

Black flies transmit Onchocerca species of cattle and deer, Dirofilaria of bears and Splendidofilaria of ducks (Srisuka et al. 2017; Aupalee et al. 2026).

Bird blood parasites

Leucocytozoon and Trypanosoma are transmitted to birds, including poultry, by bird-feeding black flies (Srisuka et al. 2017; Aupalee et al. 2026).

Cryptic species and cytotaxonomy

Black flies are renowned for extreme structural uniformity hiding abundant cryptic (isomorphic or sibling) species (Adler et al. 2016; Low et al. 2016). Morphologically indistinguishable sibling species within a single named morphospecies often differ sharply in stream habitat, host preference and vector competence, making their resolution vital for ecology and disease control (Low et al. 2016; Putt et al. 2023).

  • Polytene (giant) chromosomes: The larval salivary glands contain large polytene chromosomes whose intricate banding patterns reveal fixed and sex-linked paracentric inversions and other rearrangements. Comparing these rearrangements in sympatry provides direct evidence of reproductive isolation and forms a cornerstone of simuliid systematics (Adler et al. 2016).
  • Complexes and cytoforms: In the World Inventory, a morphospecies containing cryptic taxa is labelled a species complex. Chromosomally characterised lineages are termed cytoforms (covering both cytotypes of unresolved status and cytospecies where reproductive isolation is inferred), while informally named lineages based on DNA or subtle morphology are termed molecular forms and morphoforms (Adler 2026).
  • Examples from Asia: Analysis of 272 larvae across six nominal species of the Simulium tuberosum group in Vietnam uncovered 88 previously unknown chromosomal rearrangements, 15 cytoforms and 9 morphoforms, with only about 5% of rearrangements shared with Thailand (Adler et al. 2016). The Simulium tani complex is the largest known species complex in the Oriental Region, spanning multiple cytoforms across Thailand, Malaysia, Vietnam and Taiwan (Low et al. 2016). In Thailand alone, at least 10 morphospecies are recognised as species complexes (Takaoka et al. 2019).
Limits of single methods: Cytotaxonomy is ordinarily restricted to mature larvae fixed in Carnoy's solution and cannot directly identify field-caught biting females (Putt et al. 2023). Modern taxonomy therefore uses an integrated approach combining morphology, larval cytogenetics and DNA sequencing (mitochondrial COI and COII plus fast-evolving nuclear genes such as ECP1 and BZF) to link adult females to larval cytoforms and delimit species boundaries (Low et al. 2016; Putt et al. 2023).

Thailand and Southeast Asia

  • Species richness in Thailand: Documented Thai diversity has expanded dramatically, from just two species (S. nigrogilvum and S. digrammicum) prior to 1984 to 40 by 2000, 82 by 2010, 110 named species in the 2019 checklist (Takaoka et al. 2019), 146 by 2024 (Aupalee et al. 2024) and at least 149 named species by 2026 (Aupalee et al. 2026). All Thai species belong to the genus Simulium across six subgenera: Asiosimulium, Daviesellum, Gomphostilbia, Montisimulium, Nevermannia and Simulium (Srisuka et al. 2017; Takaoka et al. 2019).
  • Regional ecology and neighbouring countries: A two-year nationwide survey of 58 streams in 41 Thai provinces (19,456 larvae, 57 species) recorded the highest richness in mountainous northern Thailand (40 species, peaking in the cold season), with high-elevation sites hosting unique taxa such as Montisimulium, southern populations peaking in the hot season, and central, northeastern, eastern and western streams peaking in the rainy season (Srisuka et al. 2017). Nearby Vietnam already counts 73 named species in four Simulium subgenera from only five surveyed provinces, 48 described within a decade (Putt et al. 2023).
  • Human-biting species and local parasites: Seven species are well-known human biters in Thailand: S. asakoae, S. chamlongi, S. doipuiense (complex), S. nigrogilvum, S. nodosum, S. tenebrosum (complex, formerly reported as S. rufibasis) and S. umphangense (Takaoka et al. 2019; Aupalee et al. 2024, 2026). Blood-meal DNA (cytb) studies have also confirmed human feeding by S. chumpornense, S. khelangense and members of the S. striatum species-group (Aupalee et al. 2024, 2026).
  • Disease risk in Thailand: No human infectious disease transmitted by black flies has been reported in Thailand so far (Aupalee et al. 2024). However, S. asakoae, S. nigrogilvum and S. nodosum are natural vectors of zoonotic filarial nematodes (including Onchocerca spp.), and S. asakoae, S. chumpornense and S. khelangense carry avian blood protozoa (Leucocytozoon, including L. sabrazesi, and Trypanosoma avium) (Takaoka et al. 2019; Aupalee et al. 2024, 2026).
  • Where to look next: most Thai species were described by Takaoka and co-workers with Thai collaborators; see the Thailand records, the species list and the keys page.

Control and prevention

Control targets either the aquatic larvae or the parasite in people:

  • Larviciding. Larvae are concentrated in defined stretches of running water, so treating streams with larvicides such as Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Bti) is the main way to reduce biting adults.
  • Treating people. Mass treatment with ivermectin kills O. volvulus microfilariae in the skin, which reduces disease and the parasites that black flies can pick up.
  • Personal protection. Long clothing, repellents and head nets reduce bites where black flies are abundant.

Effective surveillance and control depend on identifying the biting and vector species correctly (Putt et al. 2023; Aupalee et al. 2024, 2026).

Identification

Accurate identification of black flies usually relies on examining associated life stages: collecting mature pupae and rearing them individually in vials until adults emerge together with mature last-instar larvae from the same stream (Srisuka et al. 2017; Aupalee et al. 2024). Each stage provides complementary diagnostic characters (Takaoka et al. 2019, 2020):

StageKey diagnostic characters
Adult femaleAntennal flagellomere count (typically 9, sometimes 7 or 8), frons width, size and shape of the maxillary sensory vesicle, teeth on the mandibles and maxillae, hairing on wing veins and thorax (pleural membrane, katepisternum), leg colour and fore basitarsus proportions, tarsal claw tooth, and terminalia (genital fork, spermatheca, cercus).
Adult maleNumber of horizontal rows and vertical columns of enlarged upper-eye facets, width and colour of the hind basitarsus, shiny dorsolateral pruinose patches on the abdomen, and dissected genitalia (coxite, style, ventral plate, median sclerite and parameres).
PupaNumber, branching arrangement, length and texture of the thoracic gill filaments (or inflated gill structures), tubercles and trichomes on head and thorax, abdominal hooks, spine-combs and grapnel-like hooklets, and cocoon shape and anterodorsal projection.
Mature larvaHead capsule dorsal spots and ventral postgenal cleft shape, hypostoma apical teeth and lateral serrations, labral fan primary ray count, mandible serrations, ventral papillae, and the dark thoracic gill histoblast (which shows the pupal gill filament count).

Because females of closely related species (such as in the S. asakoae and S. tuberosum groups) are often morphologically indistinguishable, morphological keys are increasingly supplemented by DNA barcoding (mitochondrial COI and nuclear markers such as BZF and ECP1, though COI alone resolved 71% of 53 Vietnamese and regional species in Putt et al. 2023 and 98.6% among seven Thai human biters in Aupalee et al. 2024), targeted multiplex PCR (100% specificity separating S. asakoae in Aupalee et al. 2026) and wing geometric morphometrics (88.5% accuracy across seven Thai human-biting species in Aupalee et al. 2024).
